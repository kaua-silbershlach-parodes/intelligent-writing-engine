# Intelligence Writing Engine — A Guide to Techniques
A concise reference for intelligence writing: six core principles plus BLUF, source-bias awareness, judgment/evidence separation, IC decision-writing checklists, synthesis (1+1=3), internal formula, ACH, alternative scenarios, and full MCIIS grammar/style rules.

## Intelligence writing techniques

Six techniques, applied to this README itself:

1. **Clarity** — Present findings clearly to avoid ambiguity or misinterpretation. Every word should be considered, questioned, and critiqued.
2. **Conciseness** — Eliminate unnecessary words and focus on the most essential information.
3. **So What?** — Answer why this is important, why it matters, and why it deserves the reader's time. If it doesn't answer the "so what?", the product is lacking.
4. **Audience Analysis** — Tailor the content to meet the intelligence requirements and expectations of the primary (and secondary) audience.
5. **Avoid Observations** — Observations aren't analytical. Determine whether your assessment is analytical and forward-leaning, and not just an observation.
6. **Objectivity** — Check your biases. Check them again. Now have a peer or colleague review your work. Avoid any biases that could skew your analysis or impact your or your organization's reputation.

## Analyst writing techniques

Five techniques, applied to this README itself:

1. **Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)** — Put the critical information across as directly as possible, first. State the judgements in the BLUF and in the impact statement that follows; then list the evidence, as succinctly as possible, to support them.
2. **A Precise Question** — Start from a specific audience and a very precise question for the analysis to tackle: who is my audience, and what specific question does it need answered? Data dumps and murky analysis come from skipping this step.
3. **Separate Judgement from Evidence** — Distinguish your judgements from the evidence you present. Each supporting paragraph leads with a judgement as its topic sentence, followed by the relevant evidence.
4. **Acknowledge Evidentiary Bias** — Identify the biases that may be inherent in the source of the information, or that may have colored it along the chain of acquisition, and acknowledge them.
5. **Keep It Simple and Straightforward (KISS)** — Write in focused paragraphs built around a single topic sentence. Word choice is specific, and sentences stay simple and straightforward, so the audience is never lost in complexity that leads to confusion or misunderstanding.

## Decision writing techniques

Seven techniques, applied to this README itself:

1. **Persuade on Facts and Logic** — Analytic writing persuades the policymaker of the superior force of a new fact or idea. Be insightful: answer how and why, and never explain the obvious. Persuade on facts and logic, not policy.
2. **Make Every Word Count** — Bring something new: insightful, timely, and accurately sourced. Make every word count, and ensure the reader takes away the same message you intended.
3. **Know Your Customer** — Before writing, answer: who is your customer? What is the point? What is the most critical evidence? Is it compelling? Is it what the customer needs? Every paragraph should advance the story in a compelling manner.
4. **Inverted Pyramid** — Put the most important material up front, and bound the subject to a few key points.
5. **Direct Structure** — Keep it clean and stripped of clauses. Use precise language, avoid adverbs and adjectives, and ensure every sentence has a purpose: noun and verb within the first six words, with a direct object.
6. **Know, Don't Know, Assess** — Distinguish clearly between what you know, what you don't know, and what you assess to be true. Make the reasoning behind every judgment transparent, articulate the reliability of your information, and account for alternative conclusions the same evidence could support. Keep the tone balanced and objective, free of value-laden terms and advocacy, and illustrate general points with concrete examples.
7. **Review in Seven Steps** — Read the first sentence; quickly read the entire piece; read the title; check logic flow; check argumentation; line edits; reread the entire text as a final sanity check.

## Analytic thinking techniques

Nine techniques, applied to this README itself:

1. **Synthesize (1+1=3)** — Draw conclusions that are greater than the data they are based on. Interpret, do not describe. Render the complex simple, and see the forest, not just the trees.
2. **Conceptualize First** — Before writing, establish three things: a contract (the title, a pledge that creates an expectation and conveys a message), a focus (the statement of synthesis — the what and so what in one simple declarative sentence), and a case (the line of reasoning that supports it). Know your bottom line before you write, because it comes first and drives the rest of the product.
3. **The Internal Formula** — Build the case in five moves: synthesis, substantiation, perspective (loose ends, ambiguities, alternative scenarios, motivations), outlook or prospects, and implications for the consumer.
4. **Gist the Information** — Distill every piece of information to its main facts or points, in as few words as possible. Group like with like, eliminate repetition, then keep only what the consumer needs to know — never what would merely be nice to know — and order it by what the consumer would want first.
5. **Core Assertions** — Make the first sentence of each paragraph a core assertion: an analytic topic sentence the rest of the paragraph supports with evidence, secondary judgments, and contextual data last. The whole argument should be traceable by reading only the core assertions.
6. **Write on Threshold** — Write when a development gives you a hook and meets threshold: a significant departure from the norm that carries implications for your consumers. Go beyond the press and the basic facts, and add judgments or insights that are uniquely yours.
7. **Basic Principles** — Determine the big picture and put conclusions first. Organize logically, with an outline. Use specific language, think simple and concise, strive for clarity of thought, and use the active voice.
8. **Objectivity With Courage** — Protect analytic objectivity above all. Have the courage to press your conclusions where the evidence warrants, however unpopular, and the courage to recast your findings when your thinking changes or new evidence appears. Show the outcomes you considered and rejected, and why.
9. **Self-Edit by Annotation** — Annotate every paragraph: mark the core assertion, then label each supporting sentence as evidence, secondary judgment, or contextual data. Check that sentences run in decreasing order of importance, that contextual data comes last, and that the title and the statement of synthesis still hold.
