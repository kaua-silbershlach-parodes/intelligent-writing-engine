# Intelligence Writing Engine — A Guide to Techniques
A concise reference for intelligence writing: six core principles plus BLUF, source-bias awareness, judgment/evidence separation, IC decision-writing checklists and review steps, synthesis (1+1=3), internal formula, ACH, alternative scenarios, and oral briefing craft.

## Intelligence writing techniques

Six techniques, applied to this README itself:

1. **Clarity** — Present findings clearly to avoid ambiguity or misinterpretation. Every word should be considered, questioned, and critiqued.
2. **Conciseness** — Eliminate unnecessary words and focus on the most essential information.
3. **So What?** — Answer why this is important, why it matters, and why it deserves the reader's time. If it doesn't answer the "so what?", the product is lacking.
4. **Audience Analysis** — Tailor the content to meet the intelligence requirements and expectations of the primary (and secondary) audience.
5. **Avoid Observations** — Observations aren't analytical. Determine whether your assessment is analytical and forward-leaning, and not just an observation.
6. **Objectivity** — Check your biases. Check them again. Now have a peer or colleague review your work. Avoid any biases that could skew your analysis or impact your or your organization's reputation.

## Analyst writing techniques

Eleven techniques — the complete synthesis of the guide, page by page, applied to this README itself:

1. **Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)** — Get the critical information across to the consumer — the specific reader you're writing for — as directly as possible: bottom line first. To determine the BLUF, be an expert on the issues and understand your audience and what it needs to know to make well-informed decisions.
2. **A Precise Question** — The key to making the complex comprehensible is a specific audience and a very precise intelligence question for the analysis to tackle. Ask first: Who is my audience, and what specific question does it need answered? Data dumps and murky analysis almost always come from skipping this step.
3. **Timeliness** — Timeliness is crucial: the consumers of intelligence — from heads of government and senior military and executive leadership to corporate executives charged with keeping people and facilities safe — need the information fast. The BLUF structure exists because of that audience.
4. **Balance Evidence and Informed Judgements** — Writing like an analyst means balancing evidence and informed judgements — neither a pile of facts nor unsupported opinion.
5. **Impartiality Above All** — Intelligence analysis must be consistently impartial or risk losing the consumer's trust. Stay cognizant of the potential for bias to influence the analysis, and factor potential biases into the analysis and the writing.
6. **Separate Judgement from Evidence** — Distinguish your judgements from the evidence you present: judgements are stated in the BLUF and in the impact statement that follows; the evidence is then listed as succinctly as possible to support them directly.
7. **Judgement-Led Paragraphs** — Each supporting paragraph leads with a judgement as its topic sentence, followed by the relevant evidence.
8. **Acknowledge Evidentiary Bias** — Consider closely the biases inherent in the ultimate source of the information, or that may have colored it along the chain of acquisition — intentional (a politician's statement carrying a party's perspective) or diluted (a report citing statistics from a third-party site). All quality analysis does due diligence to identify and acknowledge potential bias.
9. **Keep It Simple and Straightforward (KISS)** — Write clearly and concisely: focused paragraphs built around a single topic sentence, all serving as evidence to support the BLUF. Word choice is specific, simple, and straightforward — the KISS mnemonic keeps the audience from getting lost in complex sentences that lead to confusion or misunderstanding.
10. **Rewrite Dense Writing as an Intelligence Brief** — When you're stuck in a dense paper, rewrite it as an intelligence brief: it forces you to define your judgements and your evidence and distills the points into their simplest, most logical order.
11. **The Inverted Structure** — Intelligence writing flips the conventional organization of academic writing on its head: conclusion first, support after.

## Decision writing techniques

Fourteen techniques — the complete synthesis of the style guide's recoverable content, page by page, applied to this README itself:

1. **Analytic, Not Academic** — Academic writing builds your own knowledge or demonstrates expertise, on exhaustive evidence. Analytic writing persuades the policymaker of the superior force of a new fact or idea, on exploratory evidence. Both require clear writing.
2. **Bring Something New** — Successful analytic writing is something new — not a restatement of what is already known. Be insightful: answer how and why, and never explain the obvious. Be timely, carry a compelling argument, and be accurately sourced.
3. **Persuade on Facts and Logic** — Persuasion rests on facts and logic, never on policy.
4. **Make Every Word Count** — Every word must earn its place in the piece, and the reader must come away with the same message you intended.
5. **Know Your Customer — Crucial Reminders (complete)** — Who is your customer? What is the point? What is the most critical evidence? Is it compelling? Is it what the customer needs? Every paragraph should advance the story in a compelling manner.
6. **Direct Structure** — Keep it clean, stripped of clauses, with precise language. Avoid adverbs and adjectives. Ensure every sentence has a purpose: noun and verb within the first six words, with a direct object.
7. **Inverted Pyramid** — Put the most important material up front, and bound the subject to a few key points.
8. **Writing for Decision — Threshold (complete)** — Is your message of importance to those you are writing for? Is the development new and analyzed in ways that provide new insight? Have you provided your analysis in a timely manner? Is it clear why you are writing now?
9. **Writing for Decision — Message (complete)** — Is your main point clearly and prominently stated in the title and lead sentence? Does your message go beyond the obvious? Is it clear why the US should care?
10. **Writing for Decision — Analysis (complete)** — Does your piece make judgments, not just provide facts? Does it anticipate readers' questions and provide answers to them? Have you clearly distinguished between what you know, what you don't know, and what you assess to be true? Is the reasoning behind all judgments transparent and persuasive? Is the reliability of your information clearly articulated? Could the same evidence support different or alternative conclusions — and if so, are those alternatives accounted for?
11. **Writing for Decision — Compelling Argument (complete)** — Is your piece logically and coherently organized? Have you bounded the subject, making only a few key points? Is the most important material up front? Have you provided sufficient and compelling evidence to support your judgments, with just enough detail to give the customer the flavor and texture of what is happening? Have you made sure the "connective tissue" between the analysis and implications is clear? Is your tone balanced and objective, free of value-laden terms or advocacy?
12. **Writing for Decision — Structure (complete)** — Is your title true to your piece? Does the lead contain the new development and why the US should care? Does each paragraph and sentence advance the story? Have you avoided redundancy by grouping like with like?
13. **Writing for Decision — Presentation (complete)** — Is your piece concise? Have you used precise language and illustrated general points with concrete examples? Are your sentences direct (subject and verb in the first 6–8 words) and grammatically correct? Is your paper free of typos and misspellings? Do graphics, if used, effectively complement the piece?
14. **Review in Seven Steps (complete)** — 1. Read the first sentence: do I have a clear idea of the message to take away, and a sense of where the piece is going? 2. Quickly read the entire article: is the body consistent with the first sentence, does it go beyond what a reasonably intelligent generalist would know or conclude, and does it move the reader from the present (what we know) to the future (what we think will happen)? 3. Read the title: does it accurately reflect the tenor of the piece and not promise too much — consistent with the lead sentence, but not repetitive? 4. Check logic flow: do the lead sentences of each paragraph flow logically, in the right order, with each paragraph's function clear (new fact? context? outlook? implications for the US?)? 5. Check argumentation: is each lead sentence analytic, limited to one main point per paragraph, with judgments sound and well-supported, evidence consistent with the lead sentence, and no sourcing questions? 6. Line edits: weed out extraneous detail; correct grammatical errors, typos, and awkward sentence constructions. 7. Reread the entire text: a sanity check — title and lead clear and consistent with each other and with the rest of the text, the piece flowing, concisely and precisely written, free of errors, and any graphic effectively complementing the piece.

## Analytic thinking techniques

Eighty techniques — the complete synthesis of the handbook, page by page, grouped by its parts and applied to this README itself:

**The Analyst's Craft**

1. **Arrive at a Judgment, Then Make the Case** — The core skill: quickly reach an overall judgment, make a case for it, and communicate both clearly and concisely. Clarity, brevity, and precision govern writing and speaking alike.
2. **Evaluate Raw Information Critically** — Judge each item for relevance, reliability, and weight as evidence; extract the key points from a sea of detail.
3. **Synthesize — 1+1=3** — Make characterizations that are greater than the data they're based on: interpret, not describe; render the complex simple; see the forest, not just the trees. Do not pile up detail — data dumps are not analysis, and analysts are not historians.
4. **Work the Hard Problems** — Deal with ambiguity, uncover and test assumptions, reconcile conflicting information, and guard against bias, subjectivity, deception, and politicization. Judge on incomplete, conflicting, variably reliable information: provide the best possible answer in the time available.
5. **Anticipate and Assess Implications** — Anticipate trends and developments, consider the views of others, evaluate alternative scenarios, and assess implications for your consumers.
6. **Two Master Tasks** — Conceptualize (focus, frame, and advance defensible judgments) and craft (write or speak so clearly and simply that the reader cannot possibly misunderstand — every reader comes away with exactly the same message). Know your bottom line before you write: it comes first and drives the whole product.
7. **Expository Writing** — Plain talk: straightforward, matter-of-fact communication with precise words and simple language, stressing clarity, speed, and structure — written for a busy reader who is literally in a hurry to stop reading.

**Conceptualizing the Piece**

8. **The Conceptualization Triad** — Establish three things: a contract (the title — a pledge that creates an expectation and conveys a message), a focus (the statement of synthesis: the what and so what in one simple declarative sentence), and a case (the argument that supports or unfolds from the focus). If you can't crystallize the title, you don't know what you're writing about; if you can't state the focus in one sentence, you can't begin to write. Consistency of focus: the focus delivers on the title's pledge, and the case directly supports the focus.
9. **Strict Construction** — Learn the established principles with discipline first; once strict construction is mastered, loosening up and adding frills becomes easy. Merge it with what's unique to your office, and stay flexible.
10. **Craft the Title First** — The title is the first step of conceptualization and has absolute control over the rest of the piece. Make it analytic, not descriptive — your focus in abbreviated form, in a few words. Always check the title against the lead sentence: an absolute fit. Three constructions: emphasize the geographic, emphasize the topical, or mix the two. Study your office's published titles.
11. **One Focus per Piece** — A piece can have only one focus; more than one major judgment means more than one piece. The focus formula: What? (the significant departure from the norm), So what? (its relevance), Why? (the motivations and forces behind it). The exclusion rule: the piece contains only what proves, explains, or discusses the focus — everything else is left out.
12. **Steps to Focusing** — Know your customers' concerns; ask yourself the Key Intelligence Questions; gist your information down to the most important facts; then conceptualize. Discipline yourself to answer at least What? and So what?, and combine them, spelled out, in one sentence. The 30-second test: what one message do you want the reader to come away with — the one thing you'd tell the director in 30 seconds? The faster you can arrive at a focus, the better an inferential thinker you will be considered.
13. **Find the Right Level of Generality** — Neither so broad it covers everything nor so narrow it tells no relevant story: size the umbrella to what's under it. It is a judgment call built by practice. Post your focus sentence where you can see it while you work, practice statements of synthesis daily, and keep one idea per paragraph.

**When to Write**

14. **Hook and Threshold** — You need a hook (or peg): a development that gives you the opportunity to write — a single event, a series of events, or an ongoing story; happening now, forthcoming, or predicted. It must meet threshold: a significant departure from the norm with implications for your consumers' interests.
15. **Add Something Unique** — Go beyond the press and the basic facts; provide judgments or insights that answer: What is actually going on? What does it mean? What might happen next? Self-check: What? And so what? Is there a hook? Does it meet threshold? What can I add that's unique?

**Gisting**

16. **Gist Everything First** — To gist is to evaluate raw facts critically and distill them, in as few words as possible, into intelligence relevant to your consumers. Read each item and ask: what are the main facts or points? Write them down in as few words as possible — don't worry about relevance yet; gist all of it.
17. **Organize, Filter, Order** — Organize the gists logically: like data together, repetition eliminated. Then filter by consumer relevance — keep what the consumer must know, exclude what would merely be nice to know — and order by priority: the one thing you'd tell the consumer if everything else were excluded comes first.

**Key Intelligence Questions**

18. **Three Uses** — Use the Key Intelligence Questions to select the essential information from your research, to ask yourself the questions you must answer in order to make judgments, and to decide the overall analytic message you want to communicate.
19. **The Questions (complete)** — What is new on my account? What is being done differently? Why is it happening? Who are the principal actors? What are the goals, broader concerns, and motivations of the principal actors? What factors will influence success or failure? Are the actors aware of these factors? Do they have a program or strategy to deal with the factors? What constitutes success? Or failure? What are the prospects for success? Or failure? What are the consequences for the actors, their broader concerns, your consumer, the United States, or other countries — of what is taking place now? Of success? Of failure? Where do the principal actors go from here? What are alternative scenarios and their meaning for the actors, their broader concerns, your consumer, the United States, or other countries? Adapt them to the issues you follow; the principal actors may include your own office or agency.

**Building the Case**

20. **The Internal Formula (complete)** — 1. Synthesis — the picture and bottom line: what and so what. 2. Substantiation — the evidentiary base: the specific reasons you believe your focus is true. 3. Perspective — the vantage point for broader or narrower views; tie up loose ends, address ambiguities, give alternative scenarios, discuss the actors' motivations. 4. Outlook or Prospects — forward-looking judgments in greater detail than the lead. 5. Implications — the impact of the preceding judgments on the interests of your consumer, in greater detail than the lead. Outlook and implications can be combined. As a whole, the case sets your focus in context and looks beyond your focus.
21. **Core Assertions** — The lead sentence of the piece is the major core assertion: your statement of synthesis at the highest level of generality, an exact fit with the title but not verbatim repetition. A core assertion goes beyond the paragraph's data to make a judgment about the future or provide analytic insight. Every paragraph opens with a core assertion — the most important point of that paragraph, at its highest level of generality, with absolute control over everything that follows: if you promise A, you cannot go on to talk about B, C, and D. Each core assertion must directly support the statement of synthesis. The read test: extract only the core assertions, and the meaning, flow, and logic of the piece should be clear — your core assertions constitute your paper in miniature, and a reviewer may read them first.
22. **The Inverted Pyramid Paragraph** — The basic structure of all intelligence writing, from one paragraph to a twenty-page paper: core assertion first, then support in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality — begins with a bang, ends with a whimper; two to five sentences, one main point. Version I: evidence (facts that prove the assertion, sourced as necessary), with contextual data last. Version II: secondary judgments — speculation and interpretation, flagged with phrases like "we believe." Version III: a blend — evidence usually precedes secondary judgments, contextual data comes last.
23. **Advancing an Argument** — Treat a multiparagraph piece as one large inverted pyramid (statement of synthesis first, core-assertion paragraphs in decreasing order of importance), or sequence it by the internal formula — the method most pieces use. Each section of a longer paper works as a large inverted pyramid of its own.
24. **Expanding a Paragraph into a Piece** — Draft a Version II paragraph first, then expand each secondary judgment into its own paragraph: a template for a simple piece and for each section of a longer one, keeping control of your judgments and consistency of focus.

**Writing Effective Intelligence**

25. **The 11 Basic Principles (complete)** — 1. Determine the big picture. 2. Put conclusions first. 3. Organize logically. 4. Understand different formats. 5. Use specific language. 6. Think simple and concise. 7. Strive for clarity of thought. 8. Use the active voice. 9. Self-edit. 10. Know the reader's needs. 11. Develop patience.
26. **Conclusions First, Organized Logically** — Begin with judgments or findings, then support them — the reader wants your point immediately. Present information in logical, orderly form; prepare an outline to organize your ideas tightly. Know each publication format's structural design to package information with speed and versatility.
27. **Clarity Above All** — Clarity is the single most important goal: if you fail to communicate clearly, your analysis is worthless. The test is not whether you understand it, but whether there is any possibility the reader might misunderstand. Achieve it by favoring simplicity over complexity and precise language over vague words; when the meaning isn't clear, the thinking behind it may not be clear either — never sacrifice clarity for brevity.
28. **Economize and Simplify** — Aim for succinctness: brief and concise, because the reader shouldn't have to work at reading. Use short, familiar words — 25-cent words instead of $2.50 words; be careful of words over three syllables — short sentences with the main point early, and a conversational tone, as if talking to an intelligent friend. Cut deadwood and redundancy ("close confidant," "completely surrounded," "unexpected surprise"). Avoid rhetoric, colloquialisms, jargon, and vague abstractions — be specific enough that the reader can form a mental picture.
29. **Modifiers Sparingly** — Adjectives and adverbs dilute judgments and weaken credibility. Make nouns and verbs strong enough to stand alone; for each modifier ask: Do I really need it? Can I do without it? Is the word precise enough to stand alone — and if not, can I substitute a more precise word?
30. **Test Long Sentences** — Is it too wordy? Can it be condensed, or divided into two? Avoid opening with dependent phrases: Can it go at the end? Do I really need it? Does it contain a major point that deserves its own sentence?
31. **Use the Active Voice** — Nothing writes with more vigor, emphasis, and concision: the subject acts through the verb, actor before the verb, object after. Three passive flags: a verb followed by "by" (rewrite with the doer as subject); a compound verb (ask: can I drop one?); a long verb (ask: can I substitute one more precise verb?). Write passively only for good reason: to stress the verb's object, when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or obvious, or when you need to make a particular word the subject of a sentence and can't do so conveniently with the active voice.
32. **Avoid Bloopers** — Keep writing simple and direct; learn from the catalog of draft bloopers — mixed metaphors, redundancies ("a partial decrease," "for the first time, this unprecedented step," "meet the deadline on time"), and inflated circumlocutions ("inhabitants of food-deficient countries" for hungry people).
33. **Paragraph Reminders** — Every paragraph: one of the three inverted-pyramid versions; opens with a core assertion; support proves, explains, or discusses it, in decreasing order of importance; facts sourced as necessary; one main point; two to five sentences. Trick of the trade: diagram your core assertion to find the factors the paragraph should cover, and their order.
34. **Know the Reader's Needs** — Ask of your message: "So what?" Readers need insight, judgments that help them decide, and warning about matters that may require action — tell them what they want to know and what they ought to know.
35. **Develop Patience** — The written product is a team effort; the rigor of analytic thinking and writing is why review can be difficult and time-consuming.

**Longer Papers**

36. **Telescope the Internal Formula** — A longer paper juggles levels of generality, from the statement of synthesis downward; its Summary and body are built by telescoping the internal formula out. The short form is the first basic unit: each paragraph is a section, each section a paragraph. Some longer papers are descriptive, like research papers, but aim to develop analytic thinking. Read your office's publications for style, focus, format, and organization.
37. **The Summary** — The piece in a nutshell: a miniature internal formula (all five elements), one paragraph under the heading "Summary" — not an inverted pyramid paragraph. Its lead sentence is the statement of synthesis; the remaining sentences carry the main points of the body's paragraphs, more detailed than the lead but leaving something to say. Not every Summary is five sentences or contains every element — write the one that needs to be written.
38. **Body and Sections** — The body is inverted pyramid paragraphs arranged by the internal formula; transitions live in the core assertions, not at paragraph ends. Intermediate form: pull each one-paragraph section out into a multiparagraph section — one large inverted pyramid — with a heading at every junction; a section may open with a summary paragraph (primary core assertion) supported by paragraphs with secondary core assertions. Section types: Substantiation (prove the case), Perspective (stand back, tie up loose ends, alternative scenarios, motivations), Outlook or Prospects (where the issue is going), Implications (for the consumer; can combine with Outlook). An optional background paragraph gives atmospherics — short, forward-looking, never a data dump.
39. **The Long Form** — Expand sections to six, twelve, fifteen paragraphs — whatever you need — with summary paragraphs and subheadings. The danger is repetitiveness: if you find yourself repeating, you may not have enough material. Be flexible: expand only where you have enough to say.
40. **Topic Sentence Outline** — Outline in complete, declarative, analytic sentences: title, Summary, optional background, then per section the primary core assertion followed by the paragraph core assertions, most to least important — the statement of synthesis and all core assertions in black and white, which makes writing far easier. Caveat: a good outline doesn't guarantee a good paper; problems in analysis, logic, and organization may only surface in the writing.

**The Concept Paper**

41. **Write a Concept Paper First** — Before the paper: it forces you to think long and hard, articulates why the paper is worth the time, establishes an implied contract with everyone whose help you need, serves as a blueprint, and shows your growth as an analyst. Write it after reviewing sources and existing publications, talking to your chief, senior analysts, counterparts, outside experts, and consumers, and after enough research to narrow the critical issue, construct a hypothesis, and devise a methodology — but before investing unrecoverable time on the wrong track. Concept papers don't have a set format; get examples from your office.
42. **What It Must Answer** — Main point (statement of synthesis)? Relevance for your consumer? Audience? Critical assumptions? Why now? What previous work exists and how is yours different? Any unusual sensitivities? State the alternative directions, why you're discarding them, and their implications if events work out differently. Declare your stumbling blocks — gaps in sources, methodology, or understanding — the steps you're taking to close them, and what could derail the paper.
43. **Settle the Details Up Front** — Authors and contributors (and whether they know), publication type and why, coordinating components, outside reviewers, unusual sources, target date and production milestones (be conservative), maps and graphics, spinoff opportunities — and attach a topic sentence outline with headings, paragraph topics, and all core assertions.

**Self-Editing**

44. **Check the Conceptualization** — Is the title a pledge, analytic, and the only one? Does the statement of synthesis capture what and so what in one sentence? Can you trace a succinct line of reasoning — and read the table of contents and understand the argument in its briefest form? Do the sections follow the internal formula, each a large pyramid? Can you track the argument by reading only the core assertions, and does the Summary let a reader understand the entire piece?
45. **Annotate Every Paragraph** — Mark each first sentence CA (core assertion); label every other sentence E (evidence), SJ (secondary judgment), or CD (contextual data), and be able to explain each sentence's function. For factual E and CD, add source descriptions unless you have good reason not to; for SJs, include phrases like "we judge" or "we believe." Fix the order: decreasing importance or highest-to-lowest generality, contextual data last, linking language in the core assertions.
46. **Sentence Pass and Reread** — Check every sentence: active, not passive? Succinct? Clear — simple, straightforward, precise words the reader can "see"? Grammar, pronoun agreement, spelling, smooth fit? Then reread the entire piece for flow — and after any edit, reread again: editing one sentence often changes the logic around it and introduces choppiness.

**Assessing Information Needs**

47. **Work Your Gaps Systematically** — Start from a list of the questions you need to answer and ask yourself the Key Intelligence Questions; review earlier production for insights and search electronic and hardcopy files for answers or leads; assess the information received from your sources. Consult your supervisor and senior analysts to define and fill gaps; use the reference service and databases; talk to substantive counterparts across the community; discuss tasking collectors with your requirements officer.

**Objectivity — Ethics, Pitfalls, Mind-Set**

48. **Objectivity Is Paramount** — Without objectivity, products have no value and the organization has no credibility. Primary responsibility rests with the analyst and management, but objectivity is a team effort requiring vigilance, professional judgment, leadership, and integrity at every level.
49. **Analytic Courage** — Press your opinions where the evidence warrants, no matter how unpopular the conclusions — and recast your findings when your thinking changes or new evidence appears. Never let products be distorted by individual biases, misplaced assumptions, or pressures to twist analysis for policy or operational reasons.
50. **The Best-Draft Standard** — Submit a draft that shows heavy up-front thinking and planning, sound substantiation for judgments, a clear, concise, precise, well-structured style — and demonstrates you considered other outcomes, rejected them, and why.
51. **Review, Coordination, and External Views Protect Objectivity** — Review shouldn't happen in a vacuum: meet the reviewer, show how you arrived at your judgments, your evidence, and the alternatives you rejected — and understand the logic of the challenge. Coordinate officially and informally to consider the widest range of information and judgments. Use external review (cleared outside experts; conferences with experts in and out of government) for perspectives free of policy or operational bias. Give your manager alternative views to stimulate debate over conventional wisdom, and lay out different scenarios so customers can plan for the unexpected. Joint analysis across offices stimulates cross-fertilization, combines disciplines, and eliminates duplication.
52. **Pitfalls to Avoid** — Don't fear "failure": you cannot predict effectively if you don't risk being wrong; well-reasoned analysis on the best available evidence tolerates occasional wrong calls — and when managers and coordinators have agreed with the analysis, wrong calls are the organization's responsibility, not the individual's. Don't pass the buck to collectors: you usually have more information and are trained to interpret it. Don't just look backward: history is a starting point, but circumstances change and people learn. Don't stereotype: labels are a substitute for analysis that lumps together different motives and agendas. Don't let value judgments color the analysis, and beware pride of authorship and competition — you are not the font of all wisdom.
53. **Handle Your Mind-Set** — Mind-set — the distillation of your cumulative knowledge — helps when the odds favor the status quo: timely, concise, well-argued drafts; speculation compensating for missing evidence; prediction from existing assumptions under deadline. Its danger: seeing only the expected. Counter it five ways: talk out loud to find alternative language and argumentation; think backwards from an unlikely outcome; brainstorm the possible, not only the likely; use devil's advocates long on logic and short on substance; externalize the process — separate the known from the assumed, question convenient generalizations, identify overlooked information, connect unrelated ideas.
54. **When the Lead Outcome Is Shaky** — When the most likely outcome is less than 70 percent and less likely ones could pose significant threats or opportunities, consider a number of outcomes, each with well-reasoned implications and indicators. Alternatives to prediction: focus the product narrowly on specific questions; provide insights instead of estimates, from unique collection and rigorous research; or use opportunity analysis — support the consumer can act on.

**Methodologies**

55. **What a Methodology Buys You** — A systematic way to manage information to develop knowledge or make judgments: establish a logical framework first, then add depth and rigor. Methodologies help you identify your focus, structure the problem, broaden perspective, generate alternative scenarios, recognize change and threats, make sense of confusing volume, guard against locking into one track, overcome mind-set, expose inconsistencies and collection gaps, facilitate information-sharing with other analysts and agencies, highlight areas of disagreement, relate unfolding events to developments in other places and times, and add credibility to unpopular judgments. Use one when the what and so what aren't obvious, reporting is fragmentary or rich and confusing, mind-set blocks recognizing change, or the situation presents unknowns.
56. **Simple Methodologies** — Chronology: array events in sequence to spot gaps and inconsistencies and plan monitoring. Source list: list potential and actual sources to test assumptions about bias and quality and guide collection. Event analysis: classify events and their frequency, highlight unique ones, reveal patterns and early warning. Basic influence diagram: diagram relationships and influences to see patterns and identify issues.
57. **Nontraditional Methodologies** — Opportunity analysis (work backward from the outcome your consumer desires); "what if" analysis (change a basic premise to assess a low-probability, high-impact outcome); evaluation of alternative scenarios; analysis of competing hypotheses; conflict resolution (match analytic support to each stage of negotiations); preliminary problem typology (identify where alternative scenarios fit).
58. **Formal Methodologies** — Expert opinion: checklists and analytic guides; indicators (periodic reassessment, trend and warning analysis); deception analysis (motive, opportunity, means, past practices, source reliability, cost or gain). Semiquantitative: formal influence diagrams for broad issues; public opinion polling. Quantitative models and databases: statistical analysis of real-time data for economic and military questions.
59. **Limits and Choosing** — No methodology provides absolute solutions (your judgment still counts), improves the quality of your information, eliminates uncertainty, foretells timing, or predicts phenomena as distinct from decisions. Choose by testing: Does it match my research needs and products? Require long-term commitment? Help me brainstorm? Fit my deadlines — benefits worth the effort? Address the key issues and carry the analysis further? Can I get the expertise and support needed? Does management support it?

**Alternative Scenarios**

60. **Why Alternatives** — Consumers decide best with a range of possible outcomes; they are suspicious of mainline projections and resent being locked into one course. Scenarios work as a roadmap: they state explicitly why scenarios differ and suggest pressure points where consumers could make a difference. Honor the warning function: alert customers to pitfalls even when those outcomes are less likely — and more pleasant — than your mainline assessment.
61. **When and How** — Use alternatives when you lack total confidence in assumptions or evidence, when a low-chance scenario carries significant consequences, or when consumers ask. Build them into conceptualization from the start — settled by the concept paper stage, never invented after the draft is written. If review uncovers alternatives, first try to resolve the differences reasonably; don't declare every difference a legitimate scenario. Keep them few — ideally two, more than four risks a muddle — with clear distinctions among them; invite a colleague to contribute one. Rank the outcomes with your best probability judgment, and explain the ranking with signposts and assumptions. Using alternatives effectively shows analytic maturity.

**Analysis of Competing Hypotheses**

62. **Avoid the Satisficing Trap** — Most analysts pick a favorite hypothesis, seek supporting information, and stop at the first match — but most evidence is consistent with several hypotheses, and focusing on one makes you see what you're looking for. ACH systematically examines a range of possible judgments and chooses the one you believe is correct; use it for difficult issues where you must weigh alternatives and show how you arrived at your judgment.
63. **ACH Steps 1–3** — 1. Identify all possible hypotheses: brainstorm with analysts of different perspectives; raise every possibility, however remote; evaluate none at this stage. 2. List the significant evidence — including the absence of things you'd expect to see if a hypothesis were true; absence of evidence is as important as evidence present. "Evidence" is broad: deductions, assumptions, indicators, developments. 3. Build the matrix — hypotheses across the top, evidence down the side; for each item mark consistent, inconsistent, or irrelevant to each hypothesis (double marks where significantly more or less likely). Evidence equally consistent with all hypotheses has no diagnostic value — delete it to keep the matrix manageable.
64. **ACH Steps 4–5** — 4. Refine the matrix: reconsider hypotheses (add, reword, combine) and evidence (add the factors influencing your thinking); delete unimportant or non-diagnostic evidence but keep it on a separate list as a record. 5. Refute, don't confirm: draw tentative conclusions by trying to refute hypotheses — one inconsistent item can reject a hypothesis, while consistent evidence cannot prove one. The hypothesis with the most minuses is probably least likely; ordering by minuses gives a rough ranking, though evidence weight varies. The matrix reflects your judgment, not dictates it — it is also your audit trail; if you disagree with its outcome, you left out influential factors, so put them in.
65. **ACH Steps 6–8** — 6. Test sensitivity: how dependent is your conclusion on a few critical pieces of evidence? Scrutinize them — what assumptions underlie your interpretation, are there alternative explanations, does the source have a motive to deceive, must you return to the original materials? State your assumptions and your conclusion on their validity in the paper. 7. Report all the hypotheses: discuss the relative likelihood of all of them — and the ones you rejected, and why — because decisionmakers need the full set for decisions and contingency plans. 8. Identify milestones: things to look for — and things unknown — that might indicate events are taking a different route and could change your hypothesis.

**Opportunity Analysis**

66. **The Actionable Standard** — Opportunity analysis identifies opportunities or vulnerabilities consumers could exploit to advance a policy, or dangers that could undermine it — for targeting, economic analysis, and negotiations; leadership analysis counts when it suggests leverage against foreign officials. Provide explicitly "actionable" analysis the consumer can use to make a decision — without telling the consumer what to do. An opportunity assessment identifies: institutions, groups, and leaders who support the consumer's goals; means to increase their effectiveness and prestige; challenges to their influence that could be helped to derail; vulnerabilities of the opposing side that could be exploited; and activities to win over neutral segments.
67. **Do It Well** — Analysis is "effective" when consumers rely on it, attend to your judgments, and show dependence through tasking, feedback, and guidance. Formats: a comprehensive study, an informal memorandum or briefing (a smaller chunk captures a busy consumer), or current intelligence flagging a newly exploitable factor. Think like a consumer, answer like an intelligence maker: redefine the issue in the customer's terms, and remember action officers accept risk — a one-in-five chance of turning something around may be a good investment. Don't mistake public optimism for real concerns. Inch the odds upward honestly: point to opportunities and obstacles — never distort your bottom line or cheerlead. On politically sensitive issues, assess both the promising and the discouraging signs. Show your work: indicate your research effort, special sources, or distinctive tradecraft. Done well, the work is put to good use by leaders and consumers, increased consumer guidance helps allocate limited intelligence resources, and consumer reliance strengthens your leverage for avoiding politicization.

**Review and Coordination**

68. **Survive Review: Cooperate, Don't Confront** — Discuss disagreements non-confrontationally — discussion often yields a third way superior to both draft and changes. Every major change must clarify and enhance your central message; all changes are negotiable — reject none out of hand, but don't fear disagreeing with changes that don't strengthen the piece. Always submit your best product (a shoddy draft destroys your reputation) and insist on concrete feedback at every level.
69. **Accept the Corporate Product** — In review your piece becomes a corporate product — the organization's views, not your words. Disagree only when you can argue cogently that a change weakens substance or style — but do stand up for your judgments, or the reviewer will wonder why you made them. Use the reviewer's advantages: senior contacts, expertise, a fresh perspective that catches logic flaws and omissions, and experience with house style. Put your ego aside: react to comments by how they affect the piece, not who made them; learn why each change is made; keep a sense of humor — changes made at one level may be undone at the next, and both sides make mistakes.
70. **Coordinate Early and Well** — Coordination is the substantive review of your judgments and facts by components with a stake in them; the organization is corporately accountable for both. As coordinator you're responsible for more than accuracy: contribute substance that sharpens judgments and broadens analysis. Start the dialogue with counterparts and your supervisor as early as possible — especially for longer papers; resolve problems at your level before referring them up; give maximum advance notice, because short deadlines make sound reason the casualty. Distinguish real analytic disagreement from mere format or space constraints. Coordinate research programs early: combine redundant projects, agree scope, deadlines, and division of labor, meet regularly.
71. **Coordination in Practice** — Implement agreed changes (credibility); keep a level head; resolve substantive differences by examining the source documents; keep changes pertinent — don't rewrite for style alone; make comments specific, with reasons, evidence, and suggested language; if you can't agree, add alternative interpretations and identify the facts that would resolve the issue; be generous about factual errors — coordination is the safety net; remember the process works both ways.
72. **Interpersonal and Communication Skills** — Poor interpersonal skills damage a career fastest. Build relationships at all levels: choose your battles, concede without compromising principles, treat counterparts as resources and partners, acknowledge contributors, build bridges formally and informally, look for win-win solutions. Bureaucratic savvy: professionalism (fairness, successes communicated without arrogance, understanding management's perspective), reliability (follow through, take responsibility, accept consequences), initiative (offer new ideas — and keep managers informed). See products as the organization's, ask mentors and colleagues for help at the right times, and build vertical and lateral networks. Communicate: listen actively, articulate clearly without demeaning, persuade and negotiate rather than bully or withdraw, watch your body language, use humor, adjust your style to the audience, and depersonalize issues so ideas are challenged, not people. See your role as helping your counterpart get work done — lay out the facts and analysis and argue your case cogently and diplomatically rather than rewriting their prose; coordination experiences accumulate and build (or damage) respect among counterparts.

**Giving an Intelligence Briefing**

73. **Briefing Essentials** — Communicate judgments clearly, succinctly, and precisely, in a form the audience can follow: invest an inordinate amount of time up front conceptualizing, planning, and rehearsing; meet the audience's needs rather than expecting them to adjust to yours; be concise and to the point. Format: introduction (what you'll talk about and why it's significant, your bottom line, how you'll organize it), body (substantiates the bottom line, clarifies meaning, may trace events or cause and effect), conclusion (summarizes and reinforces key points — unlike writing, oral ends with a summary — and may recommend or ask for action), tied together with transition statements. Rule of thumb: 10 percent introduction, 70 percent body, 20 percent conclusion.
74. **Prepare Like an Analyst** — Accompany the senior analyst to learn; stay flexible from impromptu to formal; keep a basic briefing file on your account, updated; use the audience's vocabulary and explain special terms; never deluge listeners with irrelevant detail; anticipate questions; develop a thick skin — you may have to deliver what listeners don't want to hear; improve one problem at a time. Treat the briefing as a corporate product: get your supervisor on board early, coordinate with all relevant components in good time, follow special procedures for special audiences, and give counterparts feedback afterward, including intelligence gained from the audience.
75. **Groundwork: Audience and Situation** — Audience checklist: What do they already know? What do they need to know? What else do they want to know? Same background or a wide variety? What positions do they hold, and what action can they take? Ages, and whether age affects the briefing? What opinions do they already hold about the subject and about you? Who might try to shoot down your arguments? Individual quirks? How will they feel at the time — hungry, anxious, tired, worried? Formal or casual group? Situation checklist: emergency or not? Canned or one-time? Group size? How much time — and does it include Q&A? One in a series — where? Setup time? When does it take place?
76. **Design the Briefing** — Decide the kind of presentation: get them to act or think your way; explain what they don't know; update them on a familiar situation; or teach them how to do something. Objective, in one sentence: "At the end of my briefing I want my audience to ___." Focus: "If my audience only remembers one thing, I want it to be ___." Choose a strategy — calm logic, scare tactics, forceful emphasis, emotional appeal, friendly informality, respectful formality — weighing audience, personality, situation, and subject. Decide on two to five main points. Organize: introduction (attention, rapport, what's in it for them, essence and purpose, bottom line, preview — tell 'em what you're going to tell 'em), body (tell 'em: each main point with support, internal transitions), conclusion (tell 'em what you told 'em: restate, conclude or recommend, wrap up), then Q&A — prepared in advance with likely questions and your answers worked out.
77. **Logistics** — Bring or arrange: projectors and transparencies or slides, cover sheets, pens, paper copies, video equipment and remote, extension cord, screen, flipchart stand, flipchart paper and pens, blackboard or whiteboard and chalk or markers, microphone, handouts, demonstration items, items to pass around the audience, plug adapters, extra bulbs, water, tissues, pointer, notepads and pens for the audience, and additional material for Q&A. Check: room size, acoustics, temperature, drafts, lighting, outlets, seating, screen position and operation, outside noise, blinds, chairs and tables. At another facility: materials to send in advance, arrival time, parking, contact person, room to report to.
78. **Rehearse — and Visualize** — Never wing it. Anxiety: remember you're the expert the audience came to learn from; breathe deeply, find quiet time, relax. Use notecards — as few as comfortable, never full sheets — numbered in case they scramble. Decide in advance what you'd cut if the previous briefer runs over; check the room beforehand; videotape a rehearsal to see yourself as the audience does. Visualize the entire presentation going well — walking in, first words, the whole briefing, questions, your seat afterward — with problems built in (dropped cards, equipment trouble, interruptions) and yourself handling them. Rehearse out loud several times; if you stumble, keep going forward, never restart; don't memorize (the first sentence of each section may help); then let go of it. Listeners usually don't know when you've forgotten something — pause calmly rather than filling silence with "ah-ah-ah."
79. **Deliver** — Fake it 'til you make it: look calm, confident, and as if you're enjoying it. Some movement during the briefing — toward visual aids, toward your audience — can dissipate anxiety and engage listeners. Keep it simple, stick to your organization, avoid digressing. Eye contact: with the requester and questioners especially, complete a sentence before shifting your gaze, cover all parts of the audience. Voice: no verbal trash; moderate pace, clearly and loudly enough for everyone; short, complete sentences — if you can't say it in one breath, it's too long; vary volume, pace, and pitch; never a low, slow monotone. Stick to the time limit. If something goes wrong, don't act flustered — calmly pick it up if you need it. Never, never: demean your briefing as boring, difficult, esoteric, too complicated, or old news; apologize without a clear, definite need; read your briefing to the audience (short quotations only, for accuracy; congressional testimony excepted); make up an answer — say you don't know, note the question, offer the answer later; put your hands in your pockets, rattle keys or money, play with a pointer, or prance about.
80. **Graphics and Feedback** — Graphics complement, never compete: simple, readable from a distance, tied to specific points, minimal words — never put everything in a graphic and read it to the audience. When you point, stop and let the audience focus, then resume; keep graphics in step with the presentation and make extra copies if one must recur. Feedback is the key to improving: find out how the briefing was received; diagnose the audience's questions (elaboration? a gap you should have covered? a misunderstanding?); ask for comments, evaluations, and suggestions; have a colleague take notes on what you said, how you said it, the reaction, the questions, and your responses; seek opportunities to brief and practice what the feedback taught you.
