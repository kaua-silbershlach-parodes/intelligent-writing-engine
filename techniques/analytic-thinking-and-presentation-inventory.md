## PAGE 6
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): The Analyst's Craft — Who We Are and What We Do (Analytic Mission)
- **Evaluate raw information critically** — Judge each item for relevance, reliability, and weight as evidence; extract the key points from a sea of detail.
- **Synthesize, don't accumulate** — Make meaningful characterizations of data as judgments greater than the data they're based on: interpret, not describe; render the complex simple; "one plus one equals three."
- **Work the hard problems** — Deal with ambiguity, uncover and test assumptions, reconcile conflicting information, and guard against bias, subjectivity, deception, and "politicization."
- **Judge on incomplete information** — It is not the analyst's job to know everything; provide the best possible answer from incomplete, conflicting, variably reliable information in the time available.
- **No data dumps** — Do not pile up detail to show expertise; analysts are not historians.
- **Anticipate and assess implications** — Anticipate trends and developments, consider the views of others, evaluate alternative scenarios, and assess implications for consumers.

## PAGE 7
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): The Analyst's Craft — Conceptualizing & Crafting
- **Know your bottom line before you write** — Synthesizing takes an inordinate amount of time up front; the bottom line comes first and drives the rest of the written or oral product ("see the forest, not just the trees").
- **Two master tasks** — Conceptualize (focus, frame, and advance defensible judgments) and craft (write or speak so clearly and simply that the reader cannot possibly misunderstand; every reader comes away with exactly the same message).
- **The conceptualization triad** — Establish three things: Contract (your title, a pledge that creates an expectation and conveys a message), Focus (your statement of synthesis — the what and so what in a simple declarative sentence), and Case (your argument, the line of reasoning that supports or unfolds from your focus).
- **Expository writing** — Plain talk: straightforward, matter-of-fact communication using precise words and simple language, stressing clarity, speed, and structure; written for a busy reader who is literally "in a hurry to stop reading" — never make the reader wonder what you are getting at.

## PAGE 8
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): The Ethics of Analysis
- **Objectivity is paramount** — Protecting analytic objectivity must remain a paramount goal; without objectivity, products have no value and the organization has no credibility.
- **Analytic courage** — Have the courage to press your opinions where the evidence warrants, no matter how unpopular the conclusions, and the courage to recast your findings when your thinking changes or new evidence appears.
- **Guard against distortion** — Do not allow products to be distorted by individual biases, misplaced assumptions (yours or others'), or implicit or explicit pressures to twist analysis for policy or operational reasons.
- **Responsibility is shared** — Primary responsibility rests with the analyst and the appropriate layers of management, but encouraging objectivity is a team effort requiring vigilance, professional judgment, leadership, and integrity at every level.
- **The best-draft standard** — Submit a draft that shows a great deal of up-front thinking and planning before drafting, provides sound substantiation for judgments, is written in a clear, concise, precise, well-structured style, and demonstrates you considered other outcomes, rejected them, and why.
- **Review and coordination protect objectivity** — Conducted conscientiously, review and coordination processes are the best protection against distorting analysis — though distortion can also occur during these processes.

## PAGE 9
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): The Ethics of Analysis — External Review, Alternative Views, Joint Analysis
- **Defend your analysis in review** — Review shouldn't take place in a vacuum: meet with the reviewer, be prepared to discuss how you arrived at your judgments, what your evidence is, and what alternative conclusions you rejected and why; also try to understand the logic of the reviewer's challenge.
- **Coordinate widely** — Coordinate officially with offices and individuals who have a stake in the analysis and informally with people who have expertise, so you consider the widest possible range of information and judgments; resolve objectivity conflicts the same way as in review.
- **Use external review** — In two kinds: cleared outside experts who review your draft, and conferences with experts in and out of government — both offer perspectives free of policy or operational biases and new ways of looking at your issue.
- **Give your manager alternative views** — Alternative views stimulate debate over conventional wisdom and help you and your manager arrive at a product you both accept; lay out different scenarios in finished intelligence so customers can plan for the unexpected.
- **Joint analysis** — Products involving analysts from different offices make the best use of diverse, scarce resources: they stimulate analytic cross-fertilization, combine expertise from more than one discipline, and help eliminate duplication of effort.

## PAGE 10
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): When To Write
- **You need a hook (or peg)** — A development that gives you an opportunity to write: a single event (election or coup, terrorist incident, unexpected budget cut, international financial development, large drug seizure), a series of events, or an ongoing story in your account; it can be happening now, forthcoming, or something you predict.
- **The threshold test** — The event must meet threshold: a significant departure from the norm that warrants consumers' attention because it has implications for their interests.
- **Add something unique** — Go beyond what's said in the press or what the basic facts are; provide judgments or insights answering: What is actually going on? What does it mean? What might happen next or in the future?
- **When-to-write self-check** — Ask yourself: What? And so what? Is there a hook (or peg)? Does it meet threshold? What can I add that's unique?

## PAGE 11
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Guide to Gisting
- **Gisting defined** — To gist is to evaluate raw facts critically and distill them, in as few words as possible, into intelligence relevant to your consumers' interests; it reduces sources to their main facts or points and makes information manageable.
- **Gist everything first** — Read each piece of information and ask, "What are the main facts or points in this document?"; write them down in as few words as possible in the margins or on a separate sheet — don't worry about relevance at this stage; gist all of your information.
- **Organize the gists** — Go back and organize logically: put like data together and eliminate repetition.
- **Filter by consumer relevance** — Decide which facts to keep and which to put aside by asking, "What does the consumer need to know?" (via the key intelligence questions); keep what the consumer must know and exclude what would merely be nice or interesting to know.
- **Order by consumer priority** — Ask: what new fact would the consumer want first — if you had to exclude everything else, what one thing would you tell him? Then what next, and next, down to the least important thing.

## PAGE 15
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): The Conceptualization Process
- **Three essential elements** — The conceptualization process crystallizes your main judgment and lays out your argument for it by establishing Title, Focus, and Case — you need to have each of them.
- **Title as pledge** — Your title is a pledge to the reader: if it has to do with apples, the reader expects apples, not oranges and not apples and oranges; if you can't crystallize your title, you don't know what you're writing about.
- **Focus in one sentence** — Your focus is your statement of synthesis (overall judgment, big picture, bottom line) — the crystallization of the judgment most relevant for your consumer; if you can't crystallize it into one sentence, you can't begin to write, because you don't know the judgment you're trying to prove.
- **Case as line of reasoning** — Your case consists of the facts and subordinate judgments or points you use to lay out the argument for your focus; you can't lay out an argument if you don't know what you're trying to prove.
- **Consistency of focus** — You have it when your focus delivers on the pledge in your title and your case directly supports your focus.

## PAGE 16
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Crafting Titles
- **Craft your title first** — The title is the first step of conceptualization; crafting it first forces you to zero in on the major point, and your title and focus statement have absolute control over the rest of the piece — if you can't crystallize the piece's purview in the title, you haven't done your thinking.
- **Analytic, not descriptive** — A title is a pledge that creates an expectation in the reader; it should be analytic, convey your focus (statement of synthesis) in abbreviated form, and do so in only a few words.
- **Title–lead fit check** — Always compare your title with your lead sentence: make sure what you pledge in the title is delivered in the lead sentence — they must be an absolute fit.
- **Three title constructions** — Emphasize the geographic ("Peru: Setbacks for Drug Control"); emphasize the topical ("International Finance: Problems and Prospects for Debtor Nations"); or mix the two ("The New Frankfurt Installation: Origins and Impact of Security Problems").
- **Study your office's titles** — Study publications from your office to see how it crafts titles.

## PAGE 17
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Zeroing in on the Focus
- **One focus per piece** — You must create a statement of synthesis for any information you analyze and any piece you write, and a piece can have only one focus; if you have more than one major judgment, you have more than one piece to write.
- **The focus formula** — Express the focus in a simple declarative sentence that synthesizes information into an analytic assertion, answering: What? (the significant departure from the norm — threshold — that lets you write the piece), So what? (the relevance of the event or development), Why? (the motivations, reasons, or forces behind it). Often what? and so what? fit in one sentence; why? is answered later in the piece.
- **Names for the focus** — The focus can be called the statement of synthesis, the big picture and bottom line, the what and so what, the core assertion, or the major judgment or point.
- **The exclusion rule** — Without a focus you can't organize or draft; your piece consists only of things that support — prove, explain, or discuss — your focus; any insight or information that doesn't do this must be left out.
- **Speed signals skill** — The faster you can arrive at a focus, the better an inferential thinker you will be considered.

## PAGE 18
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Steps to Focusing
- **Steps to focusing (sequence)** — (1) Know your customers' concerns so you can produce relevant intelligence; (2) use the Key Intelligence Questions to ask yourself the questions you need to answer to make judgments relevant to those issues; (3) use gisting to reduce your information to manageable amounts and identify the most important facts or points; (4) then begin the conceptualization process.
- **Answer at least What? and So what?** — Discipline yourself to answer at least two of the major questions: What? (What's the significant departure from the norm? What new and unusual thing has happened?) and So what? (What's the consequence? Why care?)
- **Find the right level of generality** — The skill is choosing the level: "The counternarcotics program is faltering" (higher generality — the country's overall program) vs. "Opium production is surging" (lower — one aspect of it); which is right depends on the story you want to tell, and a subordinate point can become a section of the piece.
- **Spell it out in one sentence** — Combine your answers to what? and so what? in one sentence and spell everything out (e.g., "Lambodialand's opium harvest is likely to reach 20 tons this year — compared with 12 last year — doubling the amount of heroin the country may smuggle to the United States").
- **The 30-second test** — If you're having trouble narrowing your focus, ask: What one message do I want the reader to come away with? If I had the attention of my agency's director for only 30 seconds, what's the one thing I'd tell him about my piece?

## PAGE 19
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Zeroing in on the Focus — Practice! / Corollary
- **Post your focus sentence** — Write your simple declarative sentence down and post it where you can refer to it: it maintains consistency of focus while you work and is a general roadmap to the entire piece, telling you what elements to cover and in what order.
- **Practice daily** — Skill comes with practice: create statements of synthesis every day, practicing on newspaper and magazine editorials (which often have more than one focus, no clear focus, or a poorly stated focus).
- **One idea per paragraph (corollary)** — Stick literally to one idea per paragraph; if you start to tell a new story or make a new judgment, put it in a new paragraph, section, or piece.

## PAGE 20
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Developing a Case: The Internal Formula
- **What a case does** — Your case proves your focus (statement of synthesis — overall judgment, big picture, bottom line, what and so what), sets your focus in context, looks beyond your focus, and addresses what's important for the consumer; the internal formula is the structure that disciplines you to zero in on your focus and forms the basis for organizing longer papers.
- **The Internal Formula (complete, 5 steps)** — 1. Synthesis: the picture/bottom line — what/so what. 2. Substantiation: the evidentiary or other substantiating base, where you prove your focus — the specific reasons why you believe your focus is true. 3. Perspective: the vantage point from which to assess broader or narrower views and insights; you can also tie up loose ends, talk about ambiguities, give alternative scenarios, or talk about the actors' motivations. 4. Outlook or Prospects: forward-looking judgments in greater detail than the lead statement of synthesis — where things will go or how they will fare. 5. Implications: assessment of the impact of the preceding judgments on the interests of the United States or a particular consumer, in greater detail than the lead statement of synthesis; sometimes "where things are going" is the implications, and you can combine the two.

## PAGE 21
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Finding the Right Level of Generality
- **The umbrella test** — Imagine being out in the rain: how big an umbrella do you need? It depends on what's under it — you may need a regular-sized umbrella, or a circus tent to cover everything that's relevant.
- **Balance general vs. detailed** — Don't write a statement of synthesis so broad it covers everything you're going to say, or so narrow you aren't telling a relevant story: for a story about ethnic strife in Europe, neither "the future of Western civilization" (too broad) nor one group's inability to plant its crops (too narrow).
- **A judgment call built by practice** — No formula exists for easily deciding the right level of generality; determining it is your judgment call, and the skill takes time and practice to develop.

## PAGE 22
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Core Assertions (Analytic Topic Sentences)
- **Lead sentence = major core assertion** — The lead sentence of your piece is your statement of synthesis: the highest level of generality in the piece and a general roadmap to it; it delivers on the contract made with the reader in your title, so title and statement of synthesis must be an exact fit — but avoid verbatim language, which feels repetitive (déjà vu).
- **A core assertion opens every paragraph** — Begin each paragraph with a core assertion, the most important point you want to make in that paragraph; whenever you start a new paragraph, look back at your statement of synthesis to make sure the core assertion directly supports it (consistency of focus).
- **The core assertion controls the paragraph** — It is at the highest level of generality in the paragraph and has absolute control over the rest of it: it goes beyond the paragraph's data to make a judgment about the future or provide analytic insight, and everything else must prove, support, or explain the point it makes — if you promise A, you can't go on to talk about B, C, and D.
- **The core-assertion read test** — The consumer can extract just the core assertions and understand the meaning, flow, and logic of the piece; judge the quality of your core assertions and your logic by doing this extraction yourself when you self-edit.

## PAGE 23
Chapter/part: The Inverted Pyramid Paragraph
- **Inverted pyramid paragraph** — The basic structure for all intelligence writing, from one paragraph to a 20-page paper: each paragraph begins with a core assertion; the remaining sentences prove, support, or explain it.
- **Order support downward** — Supporting sentences are sourced as necessary and move in decreasing order of importance, or from highest to lowest level of generality or detail: the paragraph "begins with a bang and ends with a whimper," giving the busy reader the most important thing first.
- **One main point per paragraph** — The core assertion has absolute control over the paragraph's content: if it promises to discuss A, the paragraph cannot go on to talk about B, C, and D.
- **Paragraph length** — Each paragraph can be two to five sentences.
- **Three versions** — The inverted pyramid paragraph has three versions, each varying in the kind of support it gives its core assertion.
- **Version I (evidence)** — Core assertion, then statements of evidence (E): facts that prove the core assertion, in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality, sourced as necessary; contextual data (CD) may close it — attributed facts that add texture, richness, backdrop ("oh-by-the-way" statements, not strictly evidence).

## PAGE 24
Chapter/part: The Inverted Pyramid Paragraph
- **Version II (secondary judgments)** — Core assertion (the primary judgment), then secondary judgments (SJ) that add analysis or insights on it, in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality; this is a paragraph of speculation, premises, conjecture, interpretation — alert the reader with phrases like "we believe."
- **Version III (blend)** — Core assertion, then a blend of evidence (E), secondary judgments (SJ), and contextual data (CD); E usually precedes SJs, CD comes last; all in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality, sourced as necessary.

## PAGE 25
Chapter/part: Advancing an Argument
- **Large inverted pyramid sequencing** — One way to advance a multiparagraph line of reasoning: treat the piece as one large inverted pyramid — first paragraph is the statement of synthesis, each remaining paragraph opens with a core assertion supporting it, paragraphs arranged in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality; a longer paper seldom works this way as a whole, but each section within it does.
- **Internal formula (complete)** — The other sequencing method, used in most pieces: 1. Synthesis — statement of synthesis, big picture and bottom line, the what and so what. 2. Substantiation — evidence for the statement of synthesis. 3. Perspective — broader view or insight on the focus. 4. Outlook or Prospects — forward-looking judgments in greater detail than the statement of synthesis. 5. Implications — for the United States or the consumer: assessment of the impact of the preceding judgments, in greater detail than the statement of synthesis.

## PAGE 26
Chapter/part: Expanding a Single Paragraph to a Multiparagraph Line of Reasoning
- **Expand Version II into paragraphs** — The process turns a Version II paragraph (core assertion + supporting secondary judgments) into a series of paragraphs; it is a template for a simple piece and for each section of a longer paper, advancing the line of reasoning as one large inverted pyramid paragraph.
- **Draft the Version II paragraph first** — Go through creating a Version II paragraph before writing a section of a paper: it expands basic judgments into a longer piece while keeping control over your judgments and maintaining consistency of focus.

## PAGE 28
Chapter/part: Writing Effective Intelligence — Basic Principles of Analytic Writing
- **The 11 basic principles (complete)** — 1. Determine the big picture. 2. Put conclusions first. 3. Organize logically. 4. Understand different formats. 5. Use specific language. 6. Think simple and concise. 7. Strive for clarity of thought. 8. Use the active voice. 9. Self-edit. 10. Know the reader's needs. 11. Develop patience.
- **Conclusions first** — See the "big picture" and put conclusions up front: begin with judgments or findings, then support them — you are writing for a busy reader who wants to know immediately what your point is.
- **Organize logically** — Present information in a logical and orderly way to avoid confusing the reader and causing unnecessary reiteration; prepare an outline to help you organize your ideas tightly.
- **Understand formats** — Each kind of publication has its own structural design that helps you organize your information; understand the similarities and differences to develop speed and versatility in "packaging" publications.

## PAGE 29
Chapter/part: Writing Effective Intelligence — Achieving Clarity, Brevity, and Precision
- **Precise language** — Everyone who reads what you have written should come away with the same message; choose the word or expression that conveys exactly what you have in mind. Crucial test: not whether you understand it, but whether there is any possibility the reader might misunderstand.
- **Economize on words** — Aim for succinctness, not brevity alone: be both brief and concise, because you write for busy people and don't want the reader to have to work at reading your piece.
- **Simple, familiar terms** — Use clear, familiar, simple terms and make each word count, to say as much as possible in the available space.
- **Short sentences** — Keep sentences short to make the reader's job easier and to say as much as possible in the available space.
- **Conversational tone** — Write as you would speak to an intelligent friend.
- **Avoid verbal clutter** — Avoid redundancy, rhetoric, colloquialisms, technical jargon, and vague abstractions.
- **Modifiers sparingly** — Use adjectives and adverbs sparingly; they can weaken your judgments.
- **Long words only when needed** — You sometimes can use long words and sentences, but avoid them if short ones say the same thing.
- **Clarity of thought** — Writing is thinking on paper: when the meaning of your writing is not clear, the thoughts behind the words may not be clear. Make the meaning obvious to yourself before making it clear to the reader, and don't sacrifice clarity for brevity.

## PAGE 30
Chapter/part: Writing Effective Intelligence — Basic Principles (Active Voice / Self-Edit / Know Your Readers / Patience)
- **Use the active voice** — Active voice makes writing more direct, vigorous, and concise, which makes it sound more analytic: structure sentences so the subject performs an action that the object receives ("John bounced the ball"), not so the subject receives the action ("The ball was bounced by John").
- **Passive only when needed** — Use the passive voice when you need to make a particular word the subject of a sentence and can't do so conveniently with the active voice.
- **Self-edit everything** — Revising is an essential part of writing; few writers can produce a perfect first draft, so edit all your work before you turn it in.
- **Know the reader's needs** — Ask of your message: "So what?" The reader has to be able to use it; readers look for insight, judgments that help them make decisions, and warning about matters that might require action — tell them both what they want to know and what they ought to know.
- **Develop patience** — Your written product is a team effort; understanding the rigor of analytic thinking and writing explains why the review process can be difficult and time-consuming.

## PAGE 31
Chapter/part: Achieving Clarity, Brevity, and Precision
- **Clarity is the top goal** — Clarity is the single most important goal in analytic (expository) writing: if you fail to communicate clearly, your analysis is worthless.
- **Two ways to clarity** — Achieve clarity by (1) favoring simplicity over complexity and (2) using precise language instead of vague words.
- **Short, familiar words** — Use short, familiar words — 25-cent words instead of $2.50 words — and be careful of words over three syllables.
- **Conversational tone** — Emulate a conversational tone: write as if you were talking to an intelligent friend.
- **Cut deadwood** — Get rid of words you don't need to convey your message, especially redundant language (e.g. "close confidant," "in close proximity," "completely surrounded," "unexpected surprise," "the possibility of a coup is not beyond question").
- **Modifiers sparingly (self-test)** — Adjectives and adverbs dilute judgments and weaken credibility; make nouns and verbs strong enough to work without modifiers. Ask: Do I really need this modifier? Can I do without it? Is what I'm to modify precise enough to stand alone? If not, can I substitute a more precise word?
- **Short sentences, point early** — Keep sentences short so the busy reader can quickly integrate what you're saying; put your main point as close to the beginning of the sentence as possible — the farther the actor and verb are from the beginning, the less impact the message has.

## PAGE 32
Chapter/part: Achieving Clarity, Brevity, and Precision
- **Test long sentences** — Analyze a long sentence: Is it too wordy? Can I condense it into a shorter sentence? Would it be more effective divided into two sentences?
- **Avoid opening dependent phrases** — Avoid starting sentences with dependent phrases; for each one ask: Can I put it at the end of the sentence? Do I really need it? Does it contain a major point I should put in a separate sentence?
- **Precise over general** — Avoid vague abstractions, which are not acceptable — the more abstract the word, the less a mental picture the reader can form. Be specific: say what you really mean (name the actual attacks, provinces, and effects rather than "intensifying security problems" and "long-term consequences").
- **No jargon, rhetoric, colloquialisms** — Precision of language also means avoiding jargon, rhetoric, and colloquialisms.

## PAGE 33
Chapter/part: Achieving Clarity, Brevity, and Precision — Active Voice
- **Active voice for vigor** — There is no way to write with more vigor and emphasis than the active voice, and it also makes writing more concise: the subject acts through the verb ("John bounced the ball") instead of being acted on ("The ball was bounced by John").
- **Doer before the verb** — Putting the actor before the verb and the object after it works best most of the time.
- **Passive flag: verb + "by"** — A verb followed by "by" flags the passive voice; rewrite with the doer as subject: "(P) The decision to stage the coup was made by the insurgents" → "(A) The insurgents made the decision to stage the coup."
- **Passive flag: compound verb** — Ask "Can I drop one of them?": "(P) The results are listed in the attachment" → "(A) The results are in the attachment."
- **Passive flag: long verb** — Ask "Can I substitute a more precise verb, replacing a compound verb with one different verb?": "(P) The instructions are shown in this manual" → "(A) The instructions appear in this manual."
- **Passive only for good reason** — Write passively only when you want to stress the verb's object (e.g. "The Prime Minister was wounded today in an assassination attempt"), or when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or obvious (e.g. "Presidents are elected every four years").

## PAGE 37
Chapter/part: Longer Papers
- **Levels of generality** — A longer paper has a statement of synthesis and makes a core assertion in each paragraph; it juggles levels of generality, beginning with the highest level — the statement of synthesis — and moving on down. (Some longer papers are descriptive, like research papers, but aim to develop analytic thinking.)
- **Study your office's publications** — Read your office's publications to pick up hints on writing style, defining a focus, topic sentences, format, organization, and aspects unique to your office.
- **Telescope the internal formula** — The longer paper's structure, both Summary and body, is based on the internal formula method of advancing a line of reasoning — you telescope it out: 1. Synthesis (big picture/bottom line — what/so what). 2. Substantiation (evidentiary or other substantiating base). 3. Perspective (vantage point for broader or narrower views and insights). 4. Outlook or Prospects (forward-looking judgments in greater detail than the lead statement of synthesis). 5. Implications for the United States or the consumer (impact assessment in greater detail than the lead statement of synthesis).
- **The short form** — The short form is the first basic unit in which you bring everything together — conceptualization process, internal formula, and inverted pyramid paragraphs; in the short form, each paragraph is a section and each section a paragraph.

## PAGE 38
Chapter/part: Longer Papers — Title, Summary, Body
- **Title is a contract** — The title is a contract with your reader.
- **Summary = miniature internal formula** — The Summary is the piece in a nutshell: a miniature version of the internal formula (contains elements 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5), not an inverted pyramid paragraph; it is one paragraph, preceded by the heading "Summary."
- **Summary lead = statement of synthesis** — The Summary's lead sentence is your statement of synthesis, at the highest level of generality: it says enough to get your point across but not enough to give everything away.
- **Summary sentences 2–5** — The remaining sentences are the main points from each paragraph in the body; they are not verbatim renditions of the sections' lead sentences — more detailed than the statement of synthesis, but not so detailed that they leave nothing to say in the paper.
- **Summary flexibility** — Not every Summary is five sentences long, in the same order, or contains every part of the internal formula — write the Summary that needs to be written.
- **Body construction** — The body is composed of inverted pyramid paragraphs arranged according to the internal formula; each paragraph begins with a core assertion containing a judgment or insight that supports the statement of synthesis, then proceeds in greater detail, in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality, sourced as necessary, with facts used only to provide evidence.
- **Core assertions = paper in miniature** — Your core assertions constitute your paper in miniature, just like your Summary; a reviewer may read your core assertions first to see if your piece flows clearly and logically. A core assertion is more detailed than the Summary but less specific than the rest of its paragraph.
- **Transitions live in the core assertion** — Transition language from paragraph to paragraph is in the core assertion, not at the end of the paragraph.

## PAGE 39
Chapter/part: Longer Papers — Intermediate Form
- **Telescope into the intermediate form** — Moving from short to intermediate form: pull every one-paragraph section out like a telescope into a multiparagraph section that forms one large inverted pyramid, and give every multiparagraph section a heading.
- **Headings mark junctions** — Each heading signals the reader that you're making a transition in your case; short forms don't need headings because each paragraph's first sentence makes the transition clear, but intermediate and long forms must show the reader the junctions.
- **Section summary paragraph** — The first paragraph in a section can be a summary paragraph beginning with the primary core assertion for the entire section; the remaining paragraphs support it, each with its own secondary core assertion and accompanying support.
- **Key assumption** — In order to expand from the short to the intermediate form, you must have enough to say.
- **Background paragraph (optional)** — Optional by office: a nugget of contextual data that gives atmospherics when the reader needs scene setting — not a data dump; keep it short; its lead sentence should seem forward-looking or have insight; internally an inverted pyramid paragraph in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality.

## PAGE 40
Chapter/part: Longer Papers — Section Types
- **Substantiation section** — The section where you prove your case: a short heading that is a contract with the reader, inverted pyramid paragraphs, and the entire section is one large inverted pyramid (paragraphs in decreasing order of importance or highest-to-lowest generality); you may have several substantiation sections.
- **Perspective section** — Stands back and looks at the issue from a broader standpoint or analyzes a narrow part of it; ties up loose ends; gives alternative scenarios; "why something is happening" — e.g. the motivations of the major actors — often belongs here; short heading as contract, inverted pyramid paragraphs, the whole section one large inverted pyramid.
- **Outlook or Prospects section** — Where you think the issue is going and how you think the problem will work itself out; built from inverted pyramid paragraphs, the section as a whole one large inverted pyramid.
- **Implications section** — Implications for the United States, other countries, the principal actors, or your particular consumer; Outlook and Implications can sometimes be combined into one section or one paragraph; inverted pyramid paragraphs, the section as a whole one large inverted pyramid.

## PAGE 53
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Pitfalls To Avoid and Why (Developing Analytic Objectivity)
- **Don't fear "failure"** — You cannot predict effectively if you don't risk being wrong; occasional wrong calls are tolerable when the analysis rests on the best available evidence and is well reasoned and effectively presented — and managers and coordinators who agreed share the responsibility.
- **Don't pass the buck to collectors** — Collectors are smart and well-informed, but you the analyst normally have more information and are trained to interpret it; waiting for the definitive word can keep you from predicting early enough to be useful.
- **Don't just look backward** — History is a starting point and tips off future action, but relying on it too heavily makes you overlook changed circumstances and new factors — and people learn from mistakes and may not repeat them.
- **Don't stereotype** — Typing people or issues (rightwing, leftwing, moderate, conservative, liberal) is a substitute for analysis: imprecise labels confuse the reader, and typing lumps together people with widely different motives and agendas, leading analysts and consumers to conclude falsely that they act in concert.

## PAGE 54
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Pitfalls To Avoid and Why — Distorting (continuation)
- **Keep value judgments from coloring the analysis** — Admiration for people who overcome obstacles or approval of leaders you favor can, if you are unwary, distort how you depict an event and mislead the reader.
- **Beware pride of authorship and competition** — Pride of authorship and rivalry with other people, offices, or agencies can block objective understanding; no matter how informed you are, you are not the font of all wisdom.

## PAGE 55
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Handling Mind-Set
- **Use mind-set's advantages deliberately** — Mind-set (the distillation of your cumulative factual and conceptual knowledge) helps when the odds favor the status quo: it produces timely, concise, well-argued drafts, lets speculation compensate for lack of evidence, helps you choose among incomplete and conflicting data, supports prediction from existing assumptions, and lets you meet short deadlines by relying more on judgment than evidence.
- **Counter mind-set's danger with five practices** — Its danger is seeing only the expected and missing unusual developments. Counter it: (1) talk out loud to find alternative language and argumentation; (2) think backwards from a seemingly unlikely outcome to see if the evidence could support it; (3) brainstorm the possible, not only the likely; (4) use devil's advocates — colleagues short on substantive knowledge but long on logic and inference; (5) externalize the analytic process — separate what is known from what is assumed, question convenient generalizations, identify overlooked information, and connect previously unrelated ideas.
- **Supplement mind-set when the lead outcome is shaky** — Stress the usefulness as well as the quality of production and build both tradecraft and substantive expertise; when the most likely outcome is less than 70 percent and less likely ones could pose significant threats or opportunities, consider a number of outcomes, each with well-reasoned implications and indicators.

## PAGE 56

- **Focus the product narrowly** — Answer specific questions instead of issuing predictive, high-risk assessments; policymakers and other consumers often find such papers more useful.

## PAGE 57
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Getting Started With Methodologies
- **What a methodology is** — Any systematic way to manage information in order to develop knowledge or make judgments: establish a logical analytic framework first, then use nonintuitive ways to add depth and rigor; methodologies range from simply monitoring and classifying events to computer modeling of different scenarios.
- **What methodologies buy you** — They help you: identify your focus; bring structure, discipline, and clarity to the problem; highlight new questions and issues; broaden your perspective; stimulate new insights and ideas; rethink existing lines of analysis; analyze trends and generate alternative scenarios; recognize impending change or threats; make sense of a large, confusing volume of information; guard against locking into a particular track; overcome mind-set by increasing objectivity, identifying bias, and relating events across places and times; identify logical inconsistencies and collection gaps; facilitate information-sharing and highlight disagreement; and add credibility to new or unpopular judgments.
- **When to use a methodology** — When the what and so what aren't obvious; when reporting is fragmentary or scarce and sources biased or one-sided; when reporting is rich or issues complex and confusing; when mind-set blocks recognizing change, especially where politicization is possible or the impact significant; and when a situation presents unknowns or new directions.

## PAGE 58
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Getting Started With Methodologies — Simple and Nontraditional Methodologies
- **Chronology** — Array events in sequence for an objective view of unfolding actions; it identifies information gaps and inconsistencies and helps you plan further monitoring.
- **Source list** — List both potential reporting sources and those you have actually heard from; it tests your assumptions about source bias and information quality and guides collection.
- **Event analysis** — Classify kinds of events and their frequency and highlight unique events to reveal patterns over time; it identifies developments objectively, gives early warning of change, shows inconsistencies, and helps plan alternative scenarios.
- **Basic influence diagram** — Diagram relationships, influence, or career paths to see patterns, put factors in perspective, and identify issues for further analysis.
- **Opportunity analysis (nontraditional)** — Think backward from an outcome your consumer desires to identify the steps needed to achieve it; the challenge is to address how instead of whether.
- **'What if' analysis (nontraditional)** — Change a basic premise to assess an outcome that has low probability but high impact if it happens.
- **Evaluation of alternative scenarios (nontraditional)** — Use intuition or a methodology to lay out several possible outcomes for an intelligence issue or problem.
- **Analysis of competing hypotheses (nontraditional)** — Examine the entire range of possibilities to find the most likely one.
- **Conflict resolution (nontraditional)** — Identify consumers' needs and the kind of analytic support you can provide for each stage of negotiations.
- **Preliminary problem typology (nontraditional)** — Assess complex issues and identify situations where you can use alternative scenarios.

## PAGE 59
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Getting Started With Methodologies — Formal Methodologies
- **Expert opinion methodologies** — Systematic, subjective assessment of selected criteria: checklists and analytic guides (lists of influencing factors or criteria, e.g., to assess military and insurgent force capabilities); indicators (periodic reassessment, trend analysis, warning, and comparative analysis on issues such as political change, economic reform, and instability); and deception analysis (for ambiguous or conflicting evidence — examine motive, opportunity, means, past practices, reliability of sources, and potential cost or gain).

## PAGE 60
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Getting Started With Methodologies — Limits and Choosing
- **Know what methodologies cannot do** — No methodology or decisionmaking model can provide absolute solutions (your analytic judgment is still what counts), improve the quality of the information you use (a methodology is only as good as its analytic framework, variables, data, and execution), eliminate uncertainties and developments, foretell when or how fast events will unfold, or predict phenomena as distinct from decisions and policy implementation (e.g., refugee flows as distinct from refugee policy decisions).
- **Choose a methodology by testing questions** — Does it match my research needs and give me the framework, appendix, charts, text box, or other products I need? Does it require a long-term commitment? Can it help me brainstorm? Can I finish it within production deadlines — is it an efficient use of time and are the benefits worth the effort? Can it address the key issues and carry my analysis further, reinforcing or providing a good alternative to my judgments? Can I get the outside expertise and computer support needed? Does my office management support it?

## PAGE 61
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Alternative Scenarios — Why Alternative Views?
- **Give consumers alternative views** — Rigorously consider all information and potential outcomes so consumers understand a situation fully as they decide; alternative views are warranted when you lack total confidence in your evidence or a key assumption, and when your expert "feel" for the logic of an issue says "well, it depends" — the evidence suggests more than one outcome.
- **Honor the warning function** — Alert customers to pitfalls in their way even when those outcomes are less likely than your mainline — and often more pleasant — assessment.
- **Serve how consumers actually decide** — Consumers, especially politicians, feel best served by a range of possible outcomes, are instinctively suspicious of mainline projections, and resent being locked into one course of action; a set of scenarios works as a roadmap that states explicitly why two or more scenarios differ and suggests pressure points where consumers could make a difference.
- **Accept the tension** — Alternatives can look "messy" next to simple, direct analysis, but consumers want them included where appropriate; using alternative scenarios effectively shows analytic maturity.

## PAGE 62
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Alternative Scenarios — When To Use / How To Use
- **When alternative analysis is compelling** — When you lack total confidence in the assumptions or evidence and a change in them would justify another outcome (classic "what if" analysis); when a scenario has little chance of developing but significant consequences if it does; and when consumers ask for alternative outcomes.
- **Build alternatives into conceptualization** — Make alternative views one of the basic questions going into any project, written or oral; settle on the alternatives and judge their soundness by the concept paper stage — not as you go along, and never invent new ones after the draft is written.
- **Use review differences honestly** — Review and coordination often uncover alternatives that deserve articulation rather than papering over; but first make every effort to resolve differences reasonably — don't take the easy way out and declare all differences legitimate alternative scenarios.
- **Limit and differentiate the alternatives** — Minimize their number — ideally two are best, and more than four risks a muddle — and make sure there are clear distinctions among them; invite a colleague to contribute an alternative scenario for a different perspective.
- **Rank the outcomes** — Include your best judgment of the probabilities of individual outcomes; you owe the reader a ranking of the outcomes plus an explanation — including signposts and assumptions — of that ranking.

## PAGE 63
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Analysis of Competing Hypotheses
- **Avoid the satisficing trap** — Most analysts pick their favorite hypothesis (often on gut feeling), look for information that supports it, accept the match and stop, or reject the information as bad and repeat with another judgment; focusing on one hypothesis at a time makes you see what you're looking for, and most evidence is consistent with several different hypotheses.
- **What ACH does** — Analysis of competing hypotheses systematically examines a range of possible judgments and chooses the one you believe is correct; use it for difficult issues that require carefully weighing alternative conclusions and showing how you arrived at your judgment.
- **ACH Step 1 — Identify all possible hypotheses** — Brainstorm with a group of analysts who have different perspectives; raise every possibility no matter how remote, and don't evaluate any hypothesis at this stage.
- **ACH Step 2 — List the significant evidence** — Include as evidence the absence of things you would expect to see if a hypothesis were true; the absence of evidence is as important as the evidence you can see.

## PAGE 64
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (continuation)
- **Interpret "evidence" broadly** — Evidence includes everything that affects your judgments: logical deductions, assumptions, indicators, and specific developments. Make a general list applying to the situation as a whole, then consider each hypothesis individually, noting factors that support or contradict it — and the absence of evidence.
- **ACH Step 3 — Build and work the matrix** — Create a matrix with hypotheses across the top and evidence down the side; work across the matrix (in step 5 you work down it). For each evidence item, mark it consistent, inconsistent, or irrelevant to each hypothesis (pluses/minuses, C/I/N/A, or brief words), using double pluses or minuses where an item is significantly more or less likely under one hypothesis. Evidence equally consistent with all hypotheses has no diagnostic value — deleting it keeps the matrix manageable.
- **ACH Step 4 — Refine the matrix (begins)** — Now that you see how the evidence breaks out, reconsider the hypotheses (add new ones, reword others, combine some) and reconsider the evidence (add factors that are influencing your thinking but aren't on the list).

## PAGE 65
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (continuation)
- **ACH Step 4 — Finish refining** — Delete evidence that is unimportant or has no diagnostic value, but save it on a separate list so you keep a record of what information you considered.
- **ACH Step 5 — Refute, don't confirm** — Draw tentative conclusions by trying to refute hypotheses rather than confirm them: a single piece of inconsistent evidence may reject a hypothesis, while consistent evidence cannot prove one. The hypothesis with the most minuses is probably least likely (the most pluses is not necessarily most likely); ordering by minuses gives a rough probability ranking, though evidence weight varies. The matrix must reflect your judgment, not dictate it — it also gives you an audit trail; if you disagree with its outcome, you left out influential factors, so put them in.
- **ACH Step 6 — Test sensitivity to critical evidence** — Analyze how dependent your conclusion is on a few critical pieces of evidence: what happens to the analysis if that evidence were wrong, misleading, or differently interpreted? This exposes unrecognized critical assumptions; single out the evidence that most influenced you to reject or downplay the alternative hypotheses.

## PAGE 66
Chapter/part (if identifiable from the page): Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (continuation)
- **ACH Step 6 — Scrutinize the critical evidence** — Ask what assumptions underlie your interpretation of it, whether alternative explanations or interpretations exist, whether the source has any motive for deceiving you, and whether you need to return to original source materials to check translation accuracy or someone else's interpretation. In your paper, identify the assumptions you used to interpret the evidence and state your conclusion on their validity.
- **ACH Step 7 — Report all the hypotheses** — Discuss the relative likelihood of all the hypotheses, not just the most likely one: decisionmakers need the full set for their decisions and for contingency plans if a less likely alternative proves true; also discuss the hypotheses you rejected and why you rejected them.
- **ACH Step 8 — Identify milestones** — Lay out milestones and things to look for in the future — and things unknown to you — that might indicate events are taking a different route from what you expected and could change your hypothesis.

- **Opportunity analysis (definition)** — Identifies opportunities or vulnerabilities consumers could exploit to advance a policy, or dangers that could undermine a policy; used for military targeting, economic analysis, and trade, peace, and other negotiations.

Chapter/part: Handling Review and Coordination — Surviving the Review Process
- **Cooperate, don't confront** — Keep five principles in mind to survive review; work at making the process cooperative: discuss disagreements with the reviewer non-confrontationally (discussion often yields a third way superior to both the draft and the proposed changes); be sure every major change clarifies and enhances presentation of your central message; treat all changes as negotiable — don't reject comments out of hand, but don't be afraid to disagree with comments that don't strengthen the piece.

- **Accept a corporate product** — Your piece becomes a corporate product in review: consumers read it as your organization's views, and managers/reviewers have a duty to shape its substance and style; don't waste energy preserving every word or point — disagree with a change only when you can make a cogent argument that it weakens the piece's substance or style.

- **Defend your judgments** — Don't hesitate to stand up for your judgments; if you don't, the reviewer will wonder why you made them in the first place.
- **No one's perfect — use the reviewer's advantages** — Rigorous review improves even the most gifted analyst; reviewers bring more extensive/senior contacts, substantive expertise, a fresh perspective that detects logic flaws and omissions the too-close author missed, and more experience with the organization's style and procedures.

## PAGE 73
Chapter/part: Handling Review and Coordination — Coordination Guidelines
- **Coordination (definition)** — The process in which components that work on the issues in a draft product (written or oral) substantively review its judgments and facts, because they have a stake in what you're saying (or you in theirs); the organization is corporately accountable for the judgments and facts consumers question.
- **The coordinator's duty goes beyond accuracy** — As a coordinator you are responsible for more than ensuring substance and facts are accurate: where appropriate, provide substantive contributions that sharpen the draft's judgments and broaden its analysis.
- **Start the dialogue early** — Begin dialogue with counterparts — and your supervisor — as soon as possible, especially when planning a longer paper, to get them on board and make coordination and review easier; coordination experiences accumulate and build (or damage) respect among counterparts.
- **Wrap up at your level** — Try to resolve coordination problems at your own level; if you can't, refer the problem to your supervisor.
- **Give maximum advance notice** — Giving counterparts as much advance notice as possible is essential: short deadlines skew issue resolution, and sound reason becomes the casualty when deadlines crowd out listening to others' views, examining source information, and checking other relevant data.

- **Distinguish real disagreement from format constraints** — Space constraints and rigid formats (e.g., in current intelligence) can throw analysis off more than true differences of opinion; recognize early whether you face a basic analytic disagreement or merely need to reword judgments to be clearer within the space limitations.
- **Coordinate research programs** — Touch base with counterparts on research programs to give balance and perspective to the organization's overall program: contact counterparts early in planning so redundant or similar projects can be combined; discuss scope, deadlines, and division of labor before formal planning starts and meet regularly; give counterparts reasonable time to respond; coordinate with all relevant components.
- **Put coordination into practice (rules)** — Build credibility: once you and counterparts agree on a change, implement it; keep a level head (harsh words are unprofessional and heated arguments stray from substance); resolve substantive differences by examining the source documents; keep changes pertinent (don't rewrite a section just because you dislike its style); make comments specific — give reasons, cite supporting evidence, suggest alternative language; if you can't agree on the analysis, add alternative interpretations and identify facts that would resolve the issue if available; be generous about factual errors — coordination is the safety net for catching mistakes; remember the process works both ways.

## PAGE 78
Chapter/part: Giving an Intelligence Briefing — Essentials of Effective Oral Presentation
- **Why oral skills matter** — You must communicate your judgments and expertise clearly, succinctly, and precisely, in a way your audience can easily follow — whether a supervisor, a coordination counterpart, or a high-ranking official; weak oral skills mean your analysis doesn't get across and leaves a negative impression.
- **Invest inordinate time up front** — Like analytic writing, effective oral communication requires an inordinate amount of time up front conceptualizing, planning, and rehearsing the presentation.
- **Meet the audience's needs** — Your briefing must meet the audience's needs; don't expect the audience to adjust to yours.
- **Be concise and to the point** — Keep your briefing concise and to the point.
- **Briefing format: introduction, body, conclusion + transitions** — Present your case in a format the audience can easily follow, tying introduction, body, and conclusion together with clear transition statements. The introduction tells the audience: what you're going to talk about and why it's significant; what your bottom line is; how you will organize your presentation. The body follows that organization and presents your case in detail: it substantiates your bottom line; clarifies the meaning or importance of issues; may lead the audience through a chain of events; may clarify cause-and-effect relationships; may establish a chronological or other sequence.

## PAGE 79
Chapter/part: Giving an Intelligence Briefing — Essentials of Effective Oral Presentation (continuation)
- **The conclusion** — Summarizes and reinforces your key points so listeners with a short attention span or wandering mind don't miss them; may make recommendations or ask for action; wraps up the briefing. (Oral differs from written here: when you write, you don't end with a summary.)
- **Transition statements** — Tell your audience when, how, and why you are moving to a new topic or segment; they separate your key points and help listeners follow the organization promised in the introduction. (In writing, by contrast, you put your transitions in the core assertions.)
- **The 10/70/20 rule** — As a rule of thumb, devote about 10 percent of your briefing to the introduction, 70 percent to the body, and 20 percent to the conclusion.
- **Learn by accompanying the senior analyst** — If you are new to your account or uncomfortable briefing, invite yourself along when the senior analyst briefs; watch how he or she handles problems and criticisms.
- **Be flexible** — Briefings run the gamut from informal or impromptu to formal, prepared presentations for high-level officials.
- **Keep a basic briefing file** — Keep and update a basic briefing file on your account — information, notes, and graphics — so you are prepared if the unexpected happens.
- **Use the audience's vocabulary** — Plan to use vocabulary your audience can readily understand; explain words and phrases unique to your subject — especially important for subjects the audience knows little about and for scientific, technical, or special-vocabulary topics.
- **Don't deluge with irrelevant detail** — Don't deluge your listeners with irrelevant detail.
- **Anticipate questions** — Preparation includes anticipating questions your audience might ask.
- **Develop a thick skin** — The integrity of your judgments is paramount; you may have to tell your listeners something they don't want to hear.
- **Improve one problem at a time** — Don't try to become an expert briefer overnight; know what your problems are and work on them one at a time.

## PAGE 86
Chapter/part: Giving an Intelligence Briefing — Rehearsing and Delivering a Briefing
- **Dealing with anxiety** — Remember you are there to tell your listeners something they don't know: you are an expert asked to share your knowledge, opinion, or judgments, and the audience has come to learn from you, not to shoot you down. Never wing it — spend the inordinate amount of time up front preparing and rehearsing. Just before your briefing, do deep breathing, find quiet time, or use relaxation techniques.
- **Notecards** — Make notecards for your briefing — as few as you can and still feel comfortable; don't use full-sized sheets of paper; number your notecards so you can easily put them back in order if they get scrambled.
- **Heading off problems** — While preparing, decide what you would leave out if the briefer who precedes you runs over; whenever possible, check out the room you'll brief in beforehand; have someone videotape one of your briefings — you'll see you appear calmer than you felt, and the tape is an excellent tool for zeroing in on things to improve; listeners usually don't know when you've forgotten something; don't be afraid of pausing when you lose your place — a few seconds of silence is fine, and don't fill it with detracting sounds like "ah-ah-ah"; some movement during the briefing (toward visual aids, toward your audience) can dissipate anxiety and engage listeners.

## PAGE 87
Chapter/part: Giving an Intelligence Briefing — Rehearsing and Delivering a Briefing / Giving Your Presentation
- **Visualization** — Visualize yourself giving your entire presentation and giving it well: see yourself going through every step — walking into the room, looking at the audience, going to the front, saying your first words, giving your entire briefing, answering questions, going back to your seat, watching the audience after you've finished. Build problems into the visualization and watch yourself handling them: dropping your notecards, trouble with the viewgraph machine, forgetting your place, interruptions by questions or outside noise.
- **How to rehearse** — Practice your briefing several times, out loud, even if you're alone; if you stumble, lose your place, or forget what comes next, keep going forward — don't go back and start again from the beginning. Don't memorize your presentation (though memorizing the first sentence of each section may help it flow). Don't overdo rehearsing: go through it several times out loud, then "let go of it."
- **Fake it 'til you make it** — At least try to look calm and confident and as if you're enjoying what you're doing.
- **Keep it simple and on-plan** — Keep your presentation simple, stick to your organization, and avoid digressing.
- **Eye contact** — Maintain eye contact with the audience, especially with the person who requested the briefing and anyone asking a question; don't focus your eyes over listeners' heads (you'll lose them); look — but not stare — directly at an individual, and try to complete a sentence or point before shifting your gaze; look at individuals in all parts of the audience, including those directly to your right and left.
- **Voice** — Avoid verbal trash ("ah-ah-ah," "ummm," "er-er-ah"); speak at a moderate pace and clearly, loudly enough to be heard by everyone; use short, complete sentences — if you can't say a sentence in one breath, it's too long; vary the volume, pace, and pitch of your voice; avoid a low, slow monotone.
- **Stick to the time limit** — Stick to the time limit set for your briefing; careful rehearsing will help you do this.

## PAGE 88
Chapter/part: Giving an Intelligence Briefing — Giving Your Presentation / Using Graphics / Getting Feedback
- **When something goes wrong** — Don't act flustered if something goes wrong (you drop something, knock something over, or your flipchart falls off its stand) — you'll call undue attention to it and to yourself; calmly pick it up if you need it.
- **Never, never (delivery prohibitions)** — Never demean your briefing as boring, difficult, esoteric, too complicated, old news, or anything else negative; never apologize for anything connected with your presentation unless there's a clear, definite need; never read your briefing to the audience — read only short quotations, and then just to ensure the words are accurate (congressional testimony excepted); never make up an answer to a question — if you don't know, say so, note the question, and offer to provide an answer later; never put your hands in your pockets, rattle keys or money, play with a pointer, or prance about.
- **Using graphics** — Graphics should complement — not compete with — your briefing: keep them simple, easy to read from a distance, and tied directly to specific points you're making; keep the number of words in a graphic to a minimum (don't put everything you want to say in a graphic and then read it to listeners or ask them to read it); when you point to your graphics, stop and allow the audience to focus on them, then resume talking; keep graphics in step with your presentation — don't leap ahead or back to a visual aid, and make extra copies if you need to use a particular graphic more than once.
- **Getting feedback** — Feedback is the key to improving: always find out how your briefing was received; listen to the audience's questions — particularly the requester's — and diagnose them (are they asking you to elaborate, do they reveal a gap you should have covered, or a misunderstanding of what you said?); ask for comments, evaluations, and suggestions; have an assistant or colleague take notes covering what you said, how you said it, the audience's reaction, the questions asked, and your responses; seek out opportunities to brief in order to practice what you've learned from feedback.
