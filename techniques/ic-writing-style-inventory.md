## STYLE PAGE 1

- **Analytic writing defined** — Academic writing builds your own knowledge or demonstrates expertise in a subject and is based on exhaustive evidence; analytic writing persuades the policymaker of the superior force of a new fact or idea and is based on exploratory evidence. Both require clear writing.
- **Successful writing brings something new** — Successful analytic writing is something new, not a restatement of what is already known.
- **Be insightful — answer how and why** — Be insightful: don't explain the obvious; answer how and why.
- **Be timely** — Successful writing is timely.
- **Have a compelling argument** — The writing must carry a compelling argument.
- **Persuade on facts and logic, not policy** — Persuasion rests on facts and logic, never on policy.
- **Make every word count** — Every word must earn its place in the piece.
- **Ensure the reader takes away the same message** — Write so the reader comes away with the same message you intended.
- **Accurately sourced** — Successful writing is accurately sourced.
- **Crucial reminders when writing (complete)** — 1. Who is your customer? What is the point? What is the most critical evidence? Is it compelling? Is it what the customer needs? Every paragraph should advance the story in a compelling manner.
- **Structure: keep it clean** — Keep the writing clean, stripped of clauses, with precise language.
- **Avoid adverbs and adjectives** — Strip adverbs and adjectives from analytic writing.
- **Every sentence has a purpose** — Ensure every sentence has a purpose.
- **Noun + verb within the first six words** — Put the noun and verb within the first six words of the sentence, with a direct object.
- **Inverted Pyramid Style of Writing** — The page closes by introducing the Inverted Pyramid Style of Writing as the writing structure (heading only on this page).

## STYLE PAGE 2

- **Writing for Decision — Threshold check** — Is your message of importance to those you are writing for? Is the development new and analyzed in ways that provide new insight? Have you provided your analysis in a timely manner? Is it clear why you are writing now?
- **Writing for Decision — Message check** — Is your main point clearly and prominently stated in the title and lead sentence? Does your message go beyond the obvious? Is it clear why the US should care?
- **Writing for Decision — Analysis check** — Does your piece make judgments, not just provide facts? Does it anticipate readers' questions and provide answers to them? Have you clearly distinguished between what you know, what you don't know, and what you assess to be true? Is the reasoning behind all judgments transparent and persuasive? Is the reliability of your information clearly articulated? Could the same evidence support different or alternative conclusions, and if so, are those alternatives accounted for?
- **Writing for Decision — Compelling Argument check** — Is your piece logically and coherently organized? Have you bounded the subject, making only a few key points? Is the most important material up front? Have you provided sufficient and compelling evidence to support your judgments, with just enough detail to give the customer the flavor and texture of what is happening? Have you made sure the "connective tissue" between the analysis and implications is clear? Is your tone balanced and objective, free of value-laden terms or advocacy?
- **Writing for Decision — Structure check** — Is your title true to your piece? Does the lead contain the new development and why the US should care? Does each paragraph and sentence advance the story? Have you avoided redundancy by grouping like with like?
- **Writing for Decision — Presentation check** — Is your piece concise? Have you used precise language and illustrated general points with concrete examples? Are your sentences direct (subject and verb appear in first 6–8 words) and grammatically correct? Is your paper free of typos and misspellings? Do graphics, if used, effectively complement the piece?

## STYLE PAGE 3
- **Seven Steps of Review — Step 1: Read the First Sentence** — Do I have a clear idea of what message to take away? Do I have a sense of where the piece is going?
- **Seven Steps of Review — Step 2: Quickly Read the Entire Article** — Is the body of the piece consistent with the first sentence? Does the analysis go beyond what a reasonably intelligent generalist would know or conclude? Does it move the reader from the present (what we know) to the future (what we think will happen)?
- **Seven Steps of Review — Step 3: Read the Title** — Does the title accurately reflect the tenor of the piece and not promise too much? Is it consistent with the lead sentence, but not repetitive?
- **Seven Steps of Review — Step 4: Check Logic Flow** — Do the lead sentences of each paragraph flow logically? Are they in the right order? Is it clear what the function of each paragraph is (is it providing a new fact? Context? Outlook? Implications for the US?)
- **Seven Steps of Review — Step 5: Check Argumentation** — Are the lead sentences of each paragraph analytic and limited to one main point per paragraph? Are judgments sound and well-supported? Is the evidence in the paragraph consistent with the lead sentence? Are there any sourcing questions?
- **Seven Steps of Review — Step 6: Line Edits** — Weed out extraneous detail; correct any grammatical errors, typos, and awkward sentence constructions.
- **Seven Steps of Review — Step 7: Reread the Entire Text** — Sanity check: are the title and lead sentence clear and consistent with each other and with the rest of the text? Does the text flow? Is the piece concisely and precisely written and free of errors? If a graphic is used, does it effectively complement the piece?
